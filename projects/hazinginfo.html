Project Overview

HazingInfo.org is a national transparency platform that structures data on college hazing incidents. My role focused on the data architecture and transparency indicators used to standardize institutional reporting.

My Role

I worked as a Student Assistant on a multidisciplinary team composed of legal researchers, policy experts, and technical collaborators. My primary responsibility was overseeing the database architecture and supporting UX design decisions that shaped how complex institutional data could be presented clearly to the public.

I acted as a bridge between research workflows and technical implementation, ensuring that data collection practices aligned with how information would ultimately be surfaced to users.

Individual Contributions

I designed and maintained the platform’s database structure, defining how institutional records, transparency indicators, and incident documentation were standardized across thousands of entries.

A major contribution was creating extensive internal documentation outlining how to locate and verify hazing-related information across university websites. Because each institution stores policies and reports differently — often buried within difficult-to-navigate pages — consistent data collection required a repeatable research methodology.

These documentation standards enabled scalable data collection, improved consistency across contributors, and strengthened long-term sustainability of the project.

Database Architecture

Problem & Opportunity

Hazing transparency data is highly fragmented. Universities publish policies, reporting forms, and incident disclosures in inconsistent locations and formats, making cross-institution comparison nearly impossible.

The opportunity was to transform scattered institutional disclosures into a structured, searchable system that improves accountability while supporting researchers, journalists, and advocacy organizations.

A key challenge was developing a reliable process for identifying and verifying information that is often difficult to locate, inconsistently labeled, or periodically removed from institutional websites.

Process & Solution

The project followed an iterative research and design process combining data modeling, UX design, and continuous validation.

Research: I analyzed how universities structure policy and compliance information online and identified recurring navigation patterns and edge cases.

Design: Based on these findings, I helped shape UX decisions that prioritized clarity, comparability, and accessibility for public users.

Development: I implemented database standards within Airtable, created structured fields for transparency indicators, and established workflows for ongoing updates.

Iteration: The system evolved through stakeholder feedback and real-world data entry, refining both schema design and documentation practices over time.

Outcome & Next Steps

The resulting platform now serves as a centralized national resource that enables users to quickly evaluate institutional hazing transparency practices. Standardized data structures significantly improved consistency and reduced onboarding time for new contributors.

Future work includes expanding visualization tools, improving automated data validation workflows, and continuing to scale institutional coverage.

Key Takeaways

This project strengthened my understanding of how information architecture, ethical data practices, and UX design intersect in public-interest technology.

I learned how technical systems must adapt to messy real-world information environments and how thoughtful documentation can be as critical as code in building sustainable data platforms.
